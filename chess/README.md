# ♞ The Chess Room

**Chess, taught kindly.** A free chess app that runs entirely in your
browser. One tap plays the computer, and every game you play it counts:
a ladder of eight coaches, each one measured against the others, with
three stars to earn on every rung, and a rating of your own on the
scale every club already uses — so you can tell whether you are getting
better. Around that: a patient coach who hints and explains, an Academy
of short lessons, opening stories told plainly, a real tournament clock,
a full 3D board with four piece sets (and a lovely 2D option), and
two-player that links two devices — across the table or across the
country — with a link, a QR or four letters, and remembers the score
between you. Any game can be sent as a link that opens on any phone.
No accounts, no server, no ads, nothing tracked.
Installable as a PWA; works offline; quietly announces new versions.

Part of [The Solving Room](../). No libraries, no build step, MIT.

## Standing on open source (what we adopted, what we didn't)

We surveyed the open-source chess world so nothing here reinvents a
wheel that already rolls — but everything shipped had to fit the house
rules: MIT-compatible, no build step, no runtime dependency, offline.

**Adopted:**

- **[lichess-org/chess-openings](https://github.com/lichess-org/chess-openings)**
  (CC0 public domain) → `eco.js`: all **3,807 named opening lines**
  with ECO codes, so the room can name nearly anything two players
  wander into. Regenerated by `tools/make-eco.js`, which replays every
  line through our engine before it may ship.
- **[chess.js](https://github.com/jhlywa/chess.js)** (BSD-2-Clause,
  *dev-only, never shipped*) → the independent referee in
  `tools/crosscheck.js`: hundreds of random games, tens of thousands of
  positions, demanding identical legal-move sets (as SAN) and FENs
  between our engine and theirs. perft proves our counts; this proves
  our labels.
- **[qrcodegen](https://www.nayuki.io/page/qr-code-generator-library)**
  (public-domain algorithm, via HIVEMIND's port) → the QR invites in
  `net.js`.
- **The lichess puzzle database** (CC0,
  [database.lichess.org](https://database.lichess.org/#puzzles)) →
  `tools/make-tactics.js`, a tested adapter that curates and
  engine-verifies a beginner tactics pack for a future puzzles mode.
  The data itself doesn't ship yet only because this build environment
  can't reach the download host; the tool is ready.

**Considered and deliberately not adopted:**

- **Stockfish / stockfish.wasm / lc0** — the strongest engines alive,
  but GPL-licensed (a poor fit inside an MIT repo), megabytes of WASM
  and network weights, and vastly more strength than a teaching room
  needs. Our own search is perft-proven, cross-checked against chess.js,
  and strong enough that its advice holds up — but it is honest about
  what it is: a coach, not an oracle. The difference matters more than
  the Elo. An oracle tells you the move; a coach has to be able to say
  how much the move you chose actually cost you, which is a comparison
  rather than a verdict, and no amount of borrowed strength provides it.
- **chess.js / chessops as the shipped rules engine** — would add a
  runtime dependency for something we could prove correct ourselves
  (and now cross-check against).
- **Cburnett and other classic piece art** — CC-BY-SA/GPL share-alike
  terms sit awkwardly in an MIT repo, so every piece set carved *here*
  (the three procedural 3D sets and the hand-drawn 2D set) is original
  geometry. What we did instead of adopting art was **build the door**:
  `pieces3d.js` takes a set as plain JSON, as Wavefront `.obj`, or as a
  packed mesh baked from STL, so anyone can drop a model set in without
  us shipping one. See *Bringing your own pieces* below.

- **Syzygy tablebases, polyglot books, NNUE nets** — wonderful, heavy,
  and aimed at engines rather than learners.

> **Provenance note.** The default *Openwork Spiral* set was supplied as
> STL rather than modelled here, and its origin and licence are recorded
> as `unattributed` / `unstated` in `tools/stl-set.json` because that is
> what we actually know. Every other set in the repo is original. If you
> know where these models came from, correct those two fields and
> regenerate — the licence travels with the set into the Studio, which is
> the whole point of the field existing.

## Play the computer, and a reason to

The menu used to offer six equal doors and never say which one meant
playing alone; "Play the coach" read as a lesson. And a game against the
computer ended leaving nothing behind, so there was no reason to play
another. Most people who open a chess app want one thing first, and the
room now leads with it.

**One tap.** The lobby opens on one amber button — *Play the computer* —
at a strength chosen for you: the coach nearest your score, and while
the room is still finding your level, one a step gentler, because a
first win teaches more than a first even game. You take turns with the
colours. *Choose your opponent, colour & clock* is a line underneath,
for when you want to choose.

**A number that is yours.** Above the button sits a card with your
rating, your title and a small line of where the score has been. Tap
it and *Your record* opens: the full chart, your games won, drawn and
lost, your best, your recent accuracy, the stars you hold, and the
ladder with your score against every rung. How the number is worked
out is in *The score*, below, and on that page in a short paragraph.

**A ladder to climb.** Below the button are the eight coaches, from 🌰
Acorn to 🌳 Oak, each with its rating and three stars to earn:

- ★ win against it;
- ★ win without help — no hint, no take-back;
- ★ win without help at 80% accuracy or better.

The first star says you can beat it. The second says you can beat it on
your own. The third says you beat it on your own *and* played well,
which is the one a lucky blunder by the coach cannot hand you. A coach
whose three stars you hold is marked mastered; the one you should try
next is marked next, and the strip scrolls it into view. Tap any rung
to play it.

**A day on the streak.** A day you play is a day on your streak, and a
lesson in the Academy counts as much as a game — and so does a game
with a person, across the table or nearby, once it is properly a game
(twenty plies). The longest one is kept. The rating card only mentions
it once it is two days long. There is one streak: the Academy shows the
same number, on the same calendar.

Two players on one device, a friend nearby and the three ways to learn
sit one row further down. They are not less important; they are just
not what someone opening the room alone came for.

**New game is one tap**, from the board. It only asks first when there
is something to lose — moves on the board — and then it asks in a small
card above everything: *new game*, *change opponent* (or, between two
people, the clock), or *keep playing*.
Every question in the room now asks that way. A question used to be a
toast, which a remark arriving a second later could replace under your
finger, and from the lobby it was drawn underneath the lobby. A remark
never replaces a question now, and Escape or a tap outside picks the
last, gentlest answer.

**Starting over, honestly.** Every game against the computer is rated,
clock or no clock. Walking away from a rated game you are losing is the
oldest way there is to keep a number up, so starting another is only
free while a game is young: always before your third move (a misclick
on the colour, a change of heart about the opponent), and after that as
long as the game is short and you are not clearly behind. Once it is
well under way — twenty plies, ten moves each — or the coach's last
look puts you three pawns or more behind, starting another counts as
resigning it. The room says
which of the two it is, and asks first. A rated game left on the shelf
while you did a lesson is still a game. A reload is not leaving: the
game is still there to continue.

**Moving a piece** is a tap and a tap, as it always was — or press on
one of your own men and it is picked up at once, its squares light
while you decide, and letting go on one of them plays the move. Let go
anywhere else and it goes back. A drag that starts anywhere but your
own piece still turns the 3D board, and a second finger still zooms;
most people try to drag first, and on the flat board a drag used to do
nothing at all. A piece can be picked up while the last move is still
landing, and the move you make waits for it to land; a tap in that
moment used to be dropped without a word. On a phone shared by two
people, an option on the two-player card turns the board to whoever is
to move, once the piece has landed — off by default, because someone
playing both sides alone wants the board to stay put.

## The score

A person who plays the coach a hundred times deserves to know whether
they are getting better — not a pat on the head, but a number that
means the same thing next month as it does today, on the scale every
chess site and every club already uses, so "about 900" is a sentence
they can carry out of the room. `rating.js` keeps that number. It is
pure arithmetic — no DOM, no storage of its own, no clock — and the room
hands it a finished game and gets a new score back.

**A ladder you can measure.** The coach comes in eight strengths, named
for something growing:

| | rung | rating | recipe | accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 🌰 | **Acorn** | 400 | reads 1 ply (its choices 2), 30k positions, 400 cp slack | 66% |
| 🌱 | **Sprout** | 580 | reads 2 plies, 30k positions, 300 cp slack | 71% |
| 🍀 | **Clover** | 740 | reads 2 plies, 30k positions, 190 cp slack | 76% |
| 🌿 | **Fern** | 920 | reads 2 plies, 30k positions, 130 cp slack | 84% |
| 🎋 | **Bamboo** | 1120 | reads 3 plies, 150k positions, 130 cp slack | 85% |
| 🌵 | **Cactus** | 1370 | reads 3 plies, 150k positions, 70 cp slack | 92% |
| 🌲 | **Pine** | 1560 | reads 4 plies, 200k positions, 55 cp slack | 95% |
| 🌳 | **Oak** | 1800 | searches freely, 80k positions, 10 cp slack | 98% |

The old three levels keep their ids — `sprout`, `club` and `mentor`, now
Sprout, Fern and Oak — so saved games still find them. Each rung is a
fixed recipe: how many plies it reads, how many positions it may look
at in all, and how much slack it allows itself when choosing. The
recipe counts positions rather than milliseconds, so a rung plays the
same game on a phone as on a laptop; `engine.js` grew an optional
position budget for exactly this. The slack is what makes a weak rung
weak the way a person is weak: it picks among the moves within that
many centipawns of its best, weighted toward the better end, so it is
mostly sensible and sometimes loose rather than random. The moves it
chooses among are always read at least two plies deep, so even Acorn
sees a piece that would be taken straight back; its weakness is the
slack, not blindness.

**The ratings are measured, not guessed.** `tools/calibrate.js` plays
every pair of neighbours from the same short, sound opening lines
(taken from the lichess opening table) with the colours swapped, the
clock frozen and the dice seeded, so that any game can be replayed
exactly and a busy machine changes nothing. Each score becomes an Elo
gap — the stronger rung took 86% to 94% every time. Then it has to
decide what the numbers *mean*, and that is where it has to be honest.
Engines playing engines exaggerate: an engine never has an off day, so
it collects its whole edge in every game, and stacked raw on a
newcomer's 400 the chain would put Oak above 3000. So the chain is
pinned at both ends — Acorn at 400, someone who has just learned how
the pieces move; Oak at 1800, the cautious end of where an engine of
its kind plays people — and every measured gap between them is scaled
by the same factor (×0.52). **The spacing is measured; the scale is the
one assumption**, and it is written down in `rating.js` so it can be
argued with.

There are eight rungs rather than three because the engine's strength
is not smooth. Reading three plies instead of two is a cliff, and the
whole engine against anything reading three plies is another; the old
jump from club to mentor was as wide as the bottom half of the new
ladder put together. A rung nobody can climb to is not a rung.

**The number itself** is Glicko: a rating and a deviation that says how
sure we are of it. A newcomer starts at about 800 with a wide deviation,
and the score wears a question mark until it has settled — about five
or six games — because a provisional number should look like one. A
regular's score moves by tens. Time away makes it less sure, not lower:
the deviation grows back slowly, so the first game after a long break
counts for a little more. And no single game moves the number more than
250 points. Glicko is right that a newcomer's first result says a lot,
but a first loss to Acorn would otherwise take an 800 to 330 — a number
that reads as noise, and a first game that reads as a verdict. The
deviation still narrows by the full amount, so the cap only slows a
strong or weak newcomer's arrival by a game or two.

**Help, kindly.** Hints and take-backs are part of how this room
teaches, and they stay allowed. A game you were helped through still
counts — just for less, because it says less about what you can do
alone: each take-back keeps 80% of the game's weight, each hint 90%,
and no game falls below a quarter. The weight cuts both ways — a helped
win lifts you a little, a helped loss costs you a little — so there is
nothing to game and nothing to be afraid of. The end card says how much
a game counted.

Resigning is a loss; leaving a game is covered above. Draws by any rule
are draws. Games between two people and lessons are never rated. An
opening story isn't either, but *Play on from here* at the end of one
starts a fresh rated game from that position, against the coach that
gives you a fair fight, because from there the moves are yours.

**Accuracy, the way modern sites show it.** Every move is scored by how
much of your winning chance it kept — lichess's logistic curve, so a
pawn dropped in a level game costs far more than one dropped a rook up —
and the game is a blend of a weighted mean (moves made while the game
was swinging count more) and a harmonic mean (one blunder is not
averaged away by thirty recaptures). Forced mates are held at ±10 pawns
before they reach the curve, so missing a mate while staying a queen up
costs almost nothing and walking into one costs nearly everything. The
calibration reviewed every rung's own games at depth 5 — the same
like-for-like comparison the notebook runs — and found accuracy
climbing steadily up the ladder, 66% for Acorn to 98% for Oak. Those
points became a table from accuracy to rating, which is how the end card
can say *"you played like about 1100 today"*: a second opinion beside
the result, never a replacement for it, and never part of the score.

Three things keep that sentence honest, and each one was a way it used
to flatter:

- **The opening is left out** — the first eight plies, four moves
  each. The calibration handed both sides their openings from a book, so
  those moves were nobody's accuracy, and an opening played from memory
  is a few easy points that read as a few hundred rating points on the
  steep parts of the table.
- **Eight moves of your own after that, or no number.** Six moves of
  theory and a resignation used to read as 100%. Now the card says
  plainly that the game was too short to measure.
- **Never more than 400 above the coach.** A clean win over Acorn once
  read as "about 2400". Taking the pieces Acorn hands you is the best
  move and an easy one, and a spotless win over a 400 says you are
  comfortably stronger than 400, not that you are 2400. FIDE puts the
  same limit on a performance rating for the same reason. It is not held
  from below: a rough game against Oak is still a rough game.

**Milestones, said once.** Nothing is celebrated while the score is
provisional. The first settled score is its own moment; after that a
title — Newcomer, Improver, Casual player, Club player, Strong club
player, Advanced, Expert, the names a club noticeboard would use — is
announced the first time it is reached and never again, and a personal
best is noticed each time it climbs into a new band of fifty. Beat a
rung twice in your last three meetings while your score sits 200 above
it — three wins in four, expected — and the room says you have
outgrown it, once.

The score is read back from storage just before each game is counted,
so a second tab can't write over a game this one finished, and what is
read is rebuilt field by field, so a junk save cannot strand the room.
`rating-check.js` holds all of it to account, and `engine-check.js
--slow` plays every rung against its neighbour to confirm the ladder is
still a ladder after any change to the search.

## What the room keeps

A game used to end and be gone. The number remembered that you had won;
nothing remembered the game. And the room never said what it was you
might do next, beyond the coach it suggested. Three small things fix
that, under one law, which is the same law the score keeps:

- **Every number is a fact** about chess or about turning up — a
  rating, an accuracy, a star, a feat the rules or the engine can vouch
  for, a day you played. Nothing is minted. There are no points, no
  coins, no levels beside the rating, nothing with a rarity colour.
- **Said once, kept forever.** A milestone, a feat, a step taken is
  said at the moment, on the end card, and then it lives on your
  record. The lobby points; it never celebrates.
- **Goals are derived, never assigned.** The room reads your state and
  names the nearest true step. Ignore it and nothing happens. Nothing
  expires, there are no countdowns, and a lapse is met with silence —
  no "you lost your streak", ever.
- **Games with people count as play, never as rating.** A game across
  the table or with a friend nearby is kept in the journal and is a day
  on the streak; a feat earned against a friend nearby is a feat (the
  rules are the same rules). The number never sees any of it.

**The next step.** Under the Play button, in the row it shares with
*Choose your opponent*, one line names what you are playing for right
now, picked by a fixed order of plain rules (`goals.js` writes them
down): your first game; ideas due in the Academy, when three or more
are waiting; about how many games until your rating settles (worked out
by playing draws on a copy of your score — the deviation shrinks the
same whatever the result); the star a coach within reach still holds
back — the *highest* rung no more than 300 above you, not the lowest,
so a 920 who has Acorn's three stars is sent to Bamboo rather than back
to Sprout — and which of its three stars; a title sixty points away or
less, once the score has settled; a feat you have not done that fits
your title; the next lesson; and, always, a fair game. Tap it and the
room takes you there. When a step the lobby showed you is taken, the
end card says so once — "✓ Done: beat 🌿 Fern without a hint" — and the
lobby simply shows the next one. A step shown on five different days
without moving steps aside for the next, until it changes. When the end
card has no next coach to offer, it offers the next step instead of
nothing.

**The journal.** Every finished game is kept: against a coach, two
players on one screen, a friend nearby. Its moves, the coach's marks
(`!!` to `??`) and reviews, the opening it was, the help taken, and —
once the look-back has measured it — the accuracy. The last fifty stay.
A rated game you left by starting another is kept as the loss it was
counted as. Your record's *Games* face lists them newest first; tap one
and the board becomes that game, read-only, with the lesson bar as its
navigator: ◀ ▶ (or the arrow keys, or the move list), *Copy PGN*, and
*Done*. A game you were in the middle of stays on the shelf, exactly as
a lesson leaves it, and *Continue* is still there afterwards. *Copy them
all* gives every game as PGN — the plain text every chess program
reads, with your names, the coach's rating, the opening's code, how it
ended and the coach's marks on the moves — so a game can go to a club
coach, to lichess's analysis board, or into a notebook. Nothing locked
in.

**Feats.** Twenty-nine things done on the board, each a sentence the
rules or the engine can vouch for: mate with each piece, a smothered
mate (the mated king's every neighbour its own man), a back-rank mate,
promoting or castling *with* mate, castling long, en passant, an
underpromotion; a fork, a pin or a skewer that won — read off the
teaching eyes as you played the move, which only call a fork a fork if
it wins something whatever they reply, and not off what the teacher
happened to say aloud (it stays quiet about things you have mastered);
a win with Black, in fifteen moves, from a pawn ending, without your
queen, in sixty moves or more; and, where only the coach can see it, a
comeback from three pawns down, a lost game saved, a move it marked
`!!`, a game the look-back finds no slip in, 90% accuracy, three stars
on a coach, every coach beaten, Oak beaten. A feat is earned the first
time it is true, said on the end card (three lines at most, one sound;
the rest wait quietly on your record), and kept with the date and the
game — tap it on your record's *Feats* face and that game opens on the
board. The ones not done yet are written as plain sentences, the next
few gentlest first; they are the kind of thing a coach sets as
homework, and the next step may name one when nothing nearer is
waiting. Two people on one screen earn none — both chairs are you. A
player who had won a hundred games before the cabinet existed is not
told "a first win" on the hundred-and-first: what the rating history
and the ladder already prove (a win, Oak beaten, every coach beaten,
three stars) is filled in quietly when the room opens, dated where the
history can date it, and never announced.

**Your record, in faces.** *Record · Games · Feats*, one at a time, so
the record is never longer than it was; the Record face is exactly what
it always was. A new face is a tab and a pane with the same `data-tab`
and, if it draws itself, one entry in `PROFILE_TABS` in app.js.

Each store is `localStorage` under `chessroom_` — `chessroom_games`,
`chessroom_feats`, `chessroom_goal` — rebuilt field by field by a pure
module with a `clean()` that node checks against junk, and wiped by the
recovery card's *Erase everything* with everything else (its *clear the
game in progress* keeps them). `games-check.js`,
`feats-check.js` and `goals-check.js` hold the rules to account in node;
`progress-check.js` walks it all in a real browser, on a phone and on a
laptop.

## Bringing your own pieces

The 3D board ships four sets. **Openwork Spiral** is the default — six
turned models with helical ribs around a hollow core, so you can see
straight through every piece. The other three are carved procedurally in
`pieces3d.js`: **Staunton Classic** (the tournament pattern), **Modern
Studio** (flat-shaded geometry in the Bauhaus spirit) and **Soft Nordic**
(round, thick, hand-sized). You pick one in the Studio, under *Pieces*,
and it changes mid-game like every other part of a skin. The set rides
along in the share code too, so a look you send a friend arrives with its
men as well as its colours.

There are four ways to add a fifth.

**1. As plain JSON — the way a set travels between strangers.**
A set is a whitelist of primitives with clamped numbers. Nothing is
evaluated, so a set is as safe to paste from a stranger as a skin code:

```js
Pieces3D.register({
  id: "my-set", name: "My Set", maker: "You", license: "CC0",
  faces: "n",                       // which men turn to face the opponent
  pieces: {
    p: { height: 0.68, parts: [
      { type: "lathe", profile: [[0,0], [0.25,0,"c"], [0.10,0.35], [0.09,0.45]] },
      { type: "sphere", r: 0.10, at: [0, 0.52, 0] }
    ] },
    n: { … }, b: { … }, r: { … }, q: { … }, k: { … }
  }
});
```

`type` is one of `lathe`, `sphere`, `cone`, `cylinder`, `box`, `torus`,
`arc`; every part takes `at`, `scale`, `rx`/`ry`/`rz`, `mirrorZ`, `flat`
and `shade`. A profile point ending in `"c"` is a hard edge. `mirrorZ`
reflects the part *and its placement* across z, so the same entry with
and without it gives you a matched pair — a left eye and a right one. Anything
missing, absurd or hostile is clamped or dropped — a set of nonsense
still builds something that stands on the board.

**2. From Wavefront `.obj` — the door to the open-source world.**
Most free model sets arrive as `.obj`. Point six of them at
`registerOBJ` (or `loadOBJSet`, which fetches first) and each mesh is
re-centred, stood on the board, scaled to the height the set asks for,
turned right side out if the exporter wound it inside out, and given the
same baked occlusion as everything else:

```js
Pieces3D.loadOBJSet(
  { id: "someones-ivories", name: "Someone's Ivories", maker: "…", license: "CC0" },
  { p: "sets/pawn.obj", n: "sets/knight.obj", b: "sets/bishop.obj",
    r: "sets/rook.obj",  q: "sets/queen.obj",  k: "sets/king.obj" }
).then(function (set) { /* it's on the shelf; pick it in the Studio */ });
```

Keep each piece under 65,535 vertices — one 16-bit index buffer is the
budget, and `pieces-check.js` will tell you if you've blown it. **Check
the licence before you ship someone else's models**: CC0 and MIT drop
straight in, CC-BY needs the credit line kept (that's what the `license`
field is for), and share-alike terms will not fit an MIT repo — which is
exactly why nothing of the sort ships here.

**3. From STL, baked ahead of time — how the default set got here.**
STL is what a printable set arrives as, and it is the worst possible
shape for a browser: no shared vertices, no units, no idea which way is
up, and tens of thousands of triangles per piece because it was meant for
a nozzle. The six models behind *Openwork Spiral* came to **142,000
triangles and 6.8MB** — a slideshow on a phone and a download nobody
asked for. So `tools/make-stl-set.js` does the work once, offline:

```
read → orient (STL is Z-up, the board is Y-up) → weld coincident
vertices → decimate by quadric error → stand it on the board and size it
against the rest of the family → quantise to 16 bits → write a packed set
```

29,000 triangles and 216KB gzipped come out the other end, and the
browser only has to base64-decode and multiply. It uses quadric error
decimation (Garland & Heckbert) rather than vertex clustering, because
clustering rounds off exactly the things that make a piece recognisable —
the crenellations of a rook, the ears of a knight — and refuses any
collapse that would flip a triangle, so a thinning mesh stays closed
instead of sprouting spikes. `pieces-check.js` confirms the result is
still watertight and consistently wound, which a decimator has every
opportunity to ruin.

Drop six STLs into `chess/tools/stl/`, point `stl-set.json` at them, and
run it. Only the king's height is chosen; the other five keep whatever
proportions their designer gave them.

**4. In code, with the carving tools.** `Pieces3D.kit` exposes the
workshop itself — `lathe`, `loft`, `box`, `bevelBox`, `sphere`, `cone`,
`torus`, `arcBlock`, `smoothProfile`, the `Builder`. That's how the three
house sets are built, and it's the escape hatch the JSON form
deliberately isn't. Design against
`tools/pieces-preview.html`, which lines a set up under the board's own
shader with wireframe and baked-occlusion views, and check it with
`node chess/tools/pieces-check.js`.

## How the board is lit

Everything on the 3D board is drawn three times over, and each pass
exists because the one before it could not do the job.

**The light draws first.** The key light renders the men from where it
hangs and keeps only the distances — a shadow map. WebGL 1 has no depth
textures to rely on, so the distance is packed across four 8-bit
channels and unpacked on the way back. The board itself is left out (a
flat surface shadowing itself turns into stripes) and only the back
faces are recorded, which is the standard dodge for the speckle you get
when a surface shadows itself. Nine taps, spun by a different angle on
every pixel, turn the stair-stepped edge you would otherwise get at this
resolution into a faint noise the eye reads as softness.

**Then the camera draws the room into a texture.** Light is summed in
linear space and only converted back to sRGB at the very end. That one
change does more than any effect: multiplying an sRGB colour by a light
in sRGB space is simply the wrong sum, which is what used to make the
pale squares chalky and the dark ones muddy. The surface is
Cook-Torrance — GGX for the shape of the highlight, Smith for the
shadowing between microfacets, Schlick for the way everything turns into
a mirror at a grazing angle. A skin's material now carries roughness and
metalness beside the old Blinn-Phong pair, which the 2D board still
uses. The ambient is a two-colour sky with a lamp in it, written as a
function rather than shipped as a cube map — nothing to download, and it
tints itself from whatever skin is loaded.

The board's grain is a real surface rather than a picture of one. The
same routine that paints the squares paints them again in grey as a
height field — the pattern strokes, a groove where two squares meet, a
routed step inside the frame, the coordinates engraved rather than
printed — and a Sobel pass turns that into a normal map. Because the
board is flat and axis-aligned there is no tangent basis to carry
through: x in the texture is x in the world. A board with polish on it
also reflects the men standing on it, clipped to the wood and fading as
they fall away from it.

**Last, the picture is graded.** The bright parts are cut out, blurred
twice at a quarter width, and added back as bloom; a lens fringe and a
vignette go on; an ACES curve rolls the highlights off instead of
clipping them, which is why a white king under a lamp keeps its shape;
and a little grain lands on top. Drawing into a texture first is what
buys all of it — a highlight is allowed to go brighter than white on the
way through, and the move dots and racing lines are emitted above white
on purpose so the bloom has something to find.

None of this is load-bearing. Floating-point targets, framebuffers and
the shadow map are each checked rather than assumed; when one is missing
it drops out, the tone map moves into the surface shaders, and the board
still looks like itself. Three tiers — **Full**, **Balanced**, **Simple**
— are picked from the device and can be overridden under *Lighting &
effects* in the Studio. It is a property of the machine rather than of
the look, so it is never part of a shared skin code.

`gfx-check.js` keeps all of it honest in a real browser: it asks for
pixels rather than for reassurance.

**A board that fits the hand.** The orbit camera is fitted to the canvas
every frame rather than once: on a phone held upright it climbs steeper
and reaches for a longer lens, so the far rank is a row of men a thumb
can hit (at least 37 pixels a square on a 390×844 screen) rather than a
sliver of wood on the horizon, and both boards keep clear of the player
bars above and below. A tap is answered for whatever the eye sees first:
each man stands in a cylinder of his own shape, and a finger on his head
picks him even where his head overlaps the square behind — the wood is
only asked once no man is in the way. A man can be carried as well as
tapped: pressed, he rises into the hand, follows the finger across the
board (legal squares lighting under him), and is set down from wherever
he was let go, or glides home if the square was not his to take. The
coordinates are printed in ink chosen against the pixels really under
them, so every house skin reads at 4.5:1. And **Auto** effects watch the
frame rate: if the frames come slowly the room gives something up for
the rest of the session (pixels first, then a tier; see *The lens, and
the frames it costs*), and the Studio says why.

`board-check.js` measures every one of those claims on a real canvas.

### The lens, and the frames it costs

**The print.** The last pass grades the picture the way a print is
graded: a little more shape in the middle tones (an S laid over the
curve, so black and white stay where they were), colour a touch fuller,
warmth in what is lit and a breath of cool in what is not, a vignette.
Each place also says how much of its own sky reaches into the shade.
Open sky over the park used to fill every shadow until ivory on a pale
square had no edge left; the park now asks for half of it, under a
warmer sun, and the men stand on the board again.

**Depth.** A lens looking down at a board holds the board and lets the
rest go. The band of the picture that holds the board and every man on
it is worked out from the camera each frame (the eight corners of a box
a king tall) and is never touched, because a square you cannot read is
not worth any amount of atmosphere. Outside it the picture eases into a
copy of itself blurred at a quarter of the size: the far edge of the
table and the room behind it soften, and so does the near edge under
your hands. Four small passes; off in the studio (nothing behind the
board to lose) and on the simplest tier.

**The frame.** Where the field meets the frame there is a dark line of
stringing with a pale one outside it, the frame is shaded a little
where it stands against the squares, and a chamfer round the outside
edge catches the light. Paint, not geometry. Each man's contact shade
is firmer too, so he stands on the square, not above it.

**What a frame is made of.** The men are the heavy part of a frame (the
default set is 29,000 triangles for six models, about 140,000 for a
full board) and used to be drawn three times: into the shadow map, in
the reflection, and themselves. But the map only changes when something
that casts has moved. A camera going round the board, a fresh game's
opening shot, the walk round a mated king, a phone being tilted: the
light has not moved and neither have the men. So what casts is written
down as plain numbers each frame and compared with the last map's; if
they match, the map is kept and a third of the frame's triangles are
not drawn. In the soft-shadow tier a pixel that nothing stands over,
which is most of the board, finds that out in five lookups, not twenty.

**Holding the frame rate.** When frames come slowly the first thing to
go is something nobody can point at: the board is drawn with fewer
pixels and stretched to fit, a rung at a time (86%, 74%, 64% of the
width), stopping as soon as the frames are smooth. A device that is
really struggling (under twenty-five frames a second, where a slide
judders) is not asked to wait for the ladder: it loses a tier of
effects at once as well, as it always did. One frame rate cannot be
told from another by its gaps alone, though: a phone in low-power mode
draws thirty frames a second however little it is asked to do. So the pixels
are a trial. If drawing fewer of them all the way down the ladder did
not make the frames come at least an eighth faster, they were never the
problem; they are all given back and that door is closed for the
session. A tier somebody chose in the Studio is never second-guessed
and is drawn with every pixel it has.

`perf-check.js` checks the ladder in node against made-up frame times,
and in a browser counts what a frame is made of: the map kept on a
still board and under a moving camera, drawn again when a man moves, a
frame inside its budget of draw calls and triangles, the picture
shrinking under slow frames and coming back under a cap.

### A second pass over the light

The board's grain used to be six bezier strokes a square — the drawing
of a grain, and seen close, which is how a phone sees a board, strokes.
On the carved board they were never there at all: the skin's grain and
the lens's film grain shared a name in the palette, and the lens's few
thousandths won. So the surface is a field now, in `grain.js`: a hash,
a value noise built on it, a few octaves of that stacked into fBm, and
three recipes. Wood is rings cut at a slant, their spacing drifting
along the cut, a narrow dark year-line and the soft wood between, pores
along the grain, a broad figure across the square, and a few of the
squares cut where the rings sweep through the face as arches — the
cathedral of flat-sawn wood. Marble is two families of veins wandering
through cloud, lighter on a dark stone and darker on a pale one. Linen
is a weave, the thread going over and under. Every square is its own
cut from its own place on the plank, leaned a degree or two off square;
the frame is one long plank round them. The field holds two numbers a
pixel — a tone for the paint and a relief for the height map — and both
boards sample the one field, so the carved board's normal map can never
disagree with the colour it belongs to, and the drawn board, shown from
Black's side, samples it turned round so the grain stays on its square.
Painted once a skin change, in about a tenth of a second, and kept.

The surface model learned two things a material does that one
Cook-Torrance lobe cannot say. **Light gets into ivory.** A little of it
comes back out past the terminator, which is why the shadow side of an
ivory bishop is warm rather than black, and some comes through the thin
parts, which is why a finial glows against the light. Each material in
`skins.js` carries how far light gets in; the diffuse term is wrapped
by that much, a transmission term lights the edges from behind, and the
ambient is taken a little more evenly from all round. **And a polish is
a layer.** A clear lacquer over a material is a second, glassier
highlight that the shine slider buffs up, and whatever the coat reflects
the layer under it has already lost — porcelain is porcelain because of
the coat, not the colour. The board's gloss wears one too.

The shadows harden where they touch. A lamp is not a point, so a king's
foot throws a hard shadow and his crown, a hand's height above the
board, a soft one; the old nine taps blurred both alike. On the Full
tier four wide taps first find how far away whatever is casting is, and
that distance sets the spread of the sixteen taps that follow —
percentage-closer soft shadows, written out longhand. The bloom is two
blooms: the tight one a highlight wears as a halo, and a second, an
eighth of the width, that lets a lamp warm the whole corner of the frame
it hangs in.

### The marks on the board

Everything the board says to you is light now rather than paint. The
squares a touched man can go to are pools of light with a bright heart,
and they do not simply appear: they open out from under him, the
nearest first, a few milliseconds a square, each growing in with the
smallest overshoot — over inside a quarter of a second for the far
corner, so a blitz hand is never kept waiting. Whatever he could take
is ringed in light. The man himself is lit from within at his edges, in
the skin's own colour: a flare on the touch, two slow breaths, and then
a glow that holds without asking for another frame — the same bargain
the check square makes. A man carried by hand carries the halo with
him. The square a move has just landed on is a shade brighter for a
moment than the last-move mark will be from then on, so the eye is
taken to where the move finished rather than left to find it. The hint
is the same ribbon the racing line is drawn with — tapered, soft-edged,
bent over the traffic for a knight, its tip lit — standing still. And
every landing leaves a ring: thin, pale, gone in a quarter of a second,
bigger for a knight dropped from his hop or a king's weight than for a
bishop's glide, read off the very plan the move was animated from
(`Motion.landPower`), with one under each man as he is set out at the
start of a game. The soft discs and rings are meshes whose shade
attribute — baked occlusion on a man — is alpha on a marker, so a dot
of light is a dot of light rather than a coin. The drawn board draws
all of the same, in gradients. `motion-check.js` holds the new timings
to their promises and `grain-check.js` the wood to being wood; the
render check now also asks the painted board for its grain, and the
touched man for his halo.

## Friends

Two people who play each other want three things the room used to make
hard: to get a game going when they are not in the same room, to show
each other a game, and to know who is ahead. None of it needs an
account, and none of it puts anything about a game on a server.

**A challenge that waits.** *Play a friend* → *Invite a friend* opens a
board and gives you three ways in for the other person, all of them the
same board: a link to send (it reads "Ana challenges you to a game of
chess — 10 min + 5s a move, you're Black. Tap to sit down"), a QR under
the four letters for someone across the table, and the four letters
themselves to read out. Then you don't have to sit and watch them:
*Back to the room, keep it open* puts you in the lobby with a row that
says the board is waiting, and you can play the computer meanwhile. The
moment they arrive, the computer game is put on the shelf exactly as it
stood — *Continue your game* offers it back afterwards — and the friend
game begins. The person who taps the link is told who is asking, which
clock and which colour before they are even in.

It works across the internet as well as across a table, as long as the
two networks let two phones talk directly — most do. Some pairs (some
mobile data, some office Wi-Fi) can't, and there is no relay here to
carry the game for them, so after twenty seconds of knocking the room
says that plainly instead of spinning: the same Wi-Fi always works, and
so does one phone's hotspot. Once a game is under way its board leaves
the public list of waiting boards, so nobody else can tap into it; the
four letters still let a player whose phone dropped find their way back.

**A game you can send.** Any game — finished, or the one in progress
from the lobby — becomes a link. The whole game rides in the link (the
moves, the names, the result; a sixty-move game is a few hundred
characters), so nothing is uploaded and the part after the `#` never
reaches a server. The share is a sentence first — "♟ Ana vs Sam · 1–0
(checkmate) · Italian Game · 34 moves" — and a link second, with a QR
and a PGN for anyone who wants the game in another program.

Tapping the link offers the game; it is never opened behind your back.
It opens on the board at the first move with both names in the bar:
◀ ▶ (or the arrow keys, or a tap on any move) to step through. Then the
receiving room's own coach reads it, move by move, at the same depth it
reads your own games, and its marks land in the move list as it goes —
the annotation is deliberately not in the link, so what arrives is a
second opinion rather than the sender's word about their own play.
*Play from here* takes the game over from whatever move is on the
board, with someone on the same screen or against the coach — a
friendly game, never rated, because a position from the middle of
someone else's game is not a fair start.

The same link can point at one moment instead of the whole game: *Send*
from the bar while looking at a position sends "move 23 — Black to
play. What would you do?", opened at that move, seen from the side that
has to answer. The receiver never trusts the link: every field is
rebuilt, names lose anything that could be markup, a move that is not
legal refuses the whole link, and if the board says mate the result says
mate, whatever the link claimed.

**Your people.** Every person you play on another device is remembered
on this phone: their name as they typed it, the score between you, when
you last played, and your last dozen games together, each one ready to
look through or send. There are no friend requests and no search — a
friend is someone you sat across from. Each room makes up one quiet
random id the first time it is needed and says it to the other board in
the greeting that already carried your name, so two people both called
Sam stay two people and a Sam who renames herself is still Sam. The id
crosses the direct link and nothing else; the list never leaves the
phone, *Forget* on a row forgets that person, and the recovery card's
*Erase everything* forgets everyone.

It shows where it matters. When the boards link: "Sam again! You lead
3–2." On the end card, the rematch button carries the score — and at one
game each, and only then, "Level, 1–1 — the decider?" *Your people* (from
*Play a friend*, or *You and Sam* on the end card) lists everyone in the
order you last played, the only honest order, with a *Challenge* that
opens a board "For Sam". When you open *Play a friend*, the room looks
once at the public list of waiting boards — something anyone on the site
can already see — and only says so if a board there was opened by one of
your people, or opened for you. Nothing ranks anybody, and friend games
never touch your rating.

`node chess/tools/share-check.js` · `node chess/tools/friends-check.js`

## Notation: PGN and FEN

Every chess program speaks two plain-text languages: PGN for a game and
FEN for a position. The room now speaks both, out and in (`pgn.js`).

**Out.** Wherever the moves are, one chip — *📋 PGN · FEN* — copies the
game as PGN or the position as FEN: in the lobby beside *Send this game
so far* for the game in progress, and in the bar of the journal's viewer
and the sent-game viewer. When you are looking back at an earlier move,
the FEN is the position you are looking at. The PGN is the strict kind:
the seven-tag roster in its order, `[SetUp "1"]` and `[FEN]` when the
game began from a set-up position, the coach's marks on the moves, lines
under eighty columns, the result at the end. The journal's *Copy* and a
sent game's PGN are written by the same writer, so the format lives in
one place.

**In.** *Open a game or position* (the third tile under *More ways to
play*) takes a paste, a `.pgn` file chosen or dropped on the card, and
says what it read before anything opens — "21 moves · White: anna_k ·
Black: Sam1987 · 0–1", or "A position, Black to move · 7 men". The
reader is forgiving the way a person is (tags or none, `{}` and `;`
comments, side lines however deep, `$1` and `!?`, `12...`, `0-0`, `e8Q`,
`exd6 e.p.`, a paste of several games — the first, and a note) but it
trusts nothing: every move is played on the room's own rules, and at the
first one that is not legal it stops, keeps what came before, and names
the move and the reason ("It stops at 4. Qxf7: no White queen can play
that here"). A position is checked harder than the engine's loader does:
exactly one king each, no pawn on the first or last rank, the side not to
move not in check, at most thirty-two men; castling rights and an
en-passant square the board can't have are dropped, with a note.

A game opens in the sent-game viewer — one viewer, not a third — where
the coach reads it as it reads a game sent by a friend, and *Play from
here* takes over from any move. A position opens on the same board, seen
from the side to move, with the same *Play from here*: against the coach
or two players on this screen.

**Set-up positions are honest.** A game that did not begin from the
usual position carries it everywhere the room rebuilds a board from the
moves — the save and a reload, a take-back, looking back, the move list
(which numbers from the position's own move, Black first if it is
Black's turn). It is never rated, against any coach, and it stays so
after a reload. It is not kept in the journal either: the journal, its
feats and the next step are about whole games of your own, and the end
card says so; its *Send* copies the PGN, because a game link carries
moves from the usual start only. No opening is named and no book move
hinted from a set-up position.

**Links.** `#fen=<FEN>` (spaces or lichess's underscores) and
`#pgn=<URL-encoded PGN>` open the same card, filled in — always offered
first ("📋 A link with a position, Black to move. Open it?"), never
opened behind your back, and refused politely when they don't read.

`node chess/tools/pgn-check.js`

## Where you play

A board drawn against a plain colour reads as a diagram hanging in
space: nothing under it, nothing behind it, light from nowhere in
particular. So the board is somewhere. Under *Where you play* in the
Studio there are four places — a weathered **picnic table in a sunny
park**, a **marble bistro table in a café** with the counter and its
lamps behind it, **mahogany in a grand room** with a fire lit and a
chandelier overhead, and **green leather in a library** — and the
**Studio** backdrop for anyone who liked the board on its own.

Each place is three things, all in `scenes.js`:

- **A table.** The board stands on it, with a soft contact shade
  hugging its foot and its own shadow lying across the wood or the
  marble on the side away from the light. The top is painted on a canvas
  with a height field beside it — the picnic table's planks have gaps,
  the grand room's mahogany has its stringing inlaid — and the renderer
  cuts a normal map from that, so the light catches them.
- **The world around it**, painted once as an equirectangular panorama
  (every direction, sky to floor) and blurred the way a wide aperture
  blurs a background, with points of light — sun through leaves, café
  bulbs, chandelier crystals — dabbed on afterwards as bokeh and pushed
  past white so the bloom finds them. The renderer looks it up by
  direction from the camera's own inverse, so orbiting the board walks
  you round the park. One cheat, on purpose: the camera looks steeply
  down at a board, which in honest perspective would show nothing but
  lawn or floor beyond the table, so the backdrop's elevation is lifted
  until the top of the picture finds the treetops or the lamps.
- **Its light.** The key light hangs where the sun or the lamp really
  is in the panorama, in its colour; the fill is the other side of the
  room; the ambient is the panorama's own average above the horizon. In
  the park the sun comes through leaves and in the café through the
  window bars — a cookie texture the key light is thrown through — so
  dappled light falls across the table and the men alike. A polished
  piece reflects the place itself (its upper half the sky or the
  ceiling, its skirt the table it stands on).

The flat board lies on the same table, seen from straight above.

And each place can be heard as well as seen. Under the game, if sound
is on and the Studio's *Room tone* has not been switched off, the place
is quiet in its own way: the park has a breeze that comes and goes
through the leaves, and birds somewhere off to one side; the café has
the murmur of a room full of people none of whom you can quite hear,
and now and then a cup set down on a saucer; the grand room has its
fire, a low draw up the chimney and the crackle of it; the library has
a hush, and a page turned behind you; the studio has the air and the
slow two-note pad it always had. All of it is synthesized from noise
and a few sines in `sound.js`, like everything else there, and sits
just above the threshold of noticing. Move the board and the tone fades
into the next place's; leave the tab and it stops humming to nobody.

Nothing is downloaded: every place is painted from a seeded random
stream the first time it is chosen, in about a fifth of a second, and
kept. A place is a choice about the room rather than about the board,
so it rides in your preferences and never in a shared skin code.

## How the pieces move

A chess piece is not a sprite that slides from one square to another. A
rook goes fast and stops dead. A knight is picked up, carried over
whatever is in the way, and set down. A king is heavy. A pawn takes one
deliberate step. Those differences are not decoration — they are the
first thing that tells a beginner the pieces are not interchangeable,
and they are what makes a replay worth watching rather than skipping.

`motion.js` holds that as data: per man, how long a move takes (a base
plus so many milliseconds a square), how far clear of the board it is
carried, how long it spends rising, crossing and coming down, how hard
it lands, and how far it leans into a long diagonal. The easings are
named for the hands they imitate — `step`, `arc`, `glide`, `dash`,
`heavy`. The file is pure arithmetic with no renderer in it, so the
carved board and the drawn one ask the same question at the same moment
and get the same answer; the 2D board is a simpler drawing of the same
gesture rather than a different gesture.

Four moments are worth calling out, because each one is a rule being
explained without a sentence:

- **A capture is a push, not a deletion.** The captured piece tips away
  from whatever took it, skids a little, and is off the square before
  the mover has finished arriving. En passant topples on its own square,
  which is the clearest possible explanation of the rule that confuses
  everybody.
- **A castle is one gesture by two pieces.** The rook sets off a moment
  after the king and arrives a moment before it.
- **A promotion is the only move in chess where a piece becomes a
  different piece**, so it gets the only ceremony: the pawn turns and
  goes, a ring of light opens out of the square, and the new piece grows
  in with the one overshoot in the whole file.
- **A king in check breathes** for a few seconds rather than blinking. A
  blink is an alarm, and an alarm is the one thing a beginner being
  shown a new idea does not need more of. It settles so that a board
  nobody is touching stops asking to be redrawn.

Three moments get more than a gesture — the men being set out at the
start of a game, a capture's blow, and a king who has been mated — and
they are in *The big moments*, below.

The sound of a piece being set down plays at the moment it is set down,
not at the moment you let go of it. And `prefers-reduced-motion` is not
a slower version of any of this — it is none of it.

## Weight

The difference between a board that shows you chess and one you feel
you are playing on is mostly in how the world answers you. None of
these is large, and every one of them stops: a board that is still is a
board the phone is allowed to sleep on.

- **A man you pick up rises** off his square and waits there, his shadow
  left behind on the wood; the squares he
  can reach **ripple outward** from him a ring at a time, each popping
  in with a small overshoot, so the eye is led from the piece to its
  choices instead of everything blinking on at once. When you play the
  move he sets off from the height he was holding.
- **Carried by hand, he swings**: the crown leads the way he is being
  moved, by as much as he is being hurried, and rights himself when the
  finger stops.
- **Set down, he rocks once** on his base before he is still — a capture
  lands harder than a quiet move.
- **A capture has weight.** At the instant it lands the board holds its
  breath for a few frames (hit-stop: the board's own clock stands still
  while the wall clock does not) and the camera takes the blow — it
  drops a fraction of a square and rolls a hair, then comes back through
  one small overshoot, an underdamped spring. A queen hits harder than a
  pawn. A king, queen or rook set down without taking anything gives the
  camera the smallest nudge; the light pieces land without one, so it
  means something.
- **What was taken goes somewhere.** On a table, a man just taken is
  lifted off his square and carried to a row beside the board, on the
  right hand of whoever took him, smallest first — the way people
  actually play. The row is read off the position, so it is right after
  an undo, a resumed game or a look back — and read from the game's own
  record of captures, never from the position, so a promoted pawn never
  turns a captured queen into a pawn and a lesson's sparse board has no
  phantom row.
- **A fresh game opens on a shot**: low across the board from beside
  your corner, the men arriving large in front of the place behind
  them, and the camera rising into your own view as the last of them
  land.
- **After a mate the camera walks slowly round** the fallen king, the
  way a film lingers on its last shot, and stops the moment anybody
  touches the board.
- **Tilt to look round.** On a phone the camera steps a degree or two
  the way you tip it, as if you had moved your head over a real board.
  It follows movement rather than angle and ignores the tremble of a
  hand, so a still phone is a still board.

All of it is a layer over the player's own view — none of it writes to
the orbit, so pinching and turning keep working through every one — and
all of it is off under reduced motion. The curves live in `motion.js`
as pure functions of time (`thud`, `swoop`, `settle`, `ripple`, `hold`),
so the flat board asks the same questions and gets the same answers, and
`tools/feel-check.js` checks that each one starts, and that each one
stops.

## The big moments

Most of a game of chess is quiet, and should be: a piece is picked up,
carried, set down, and the room says nothing. But a game has
punctuation too — a capture, a check, a pawn that becomes a queen, a
king with nowhere left to go — and a board that marks all of those the
same as a quiet move is a board that is not paying attention.

So there is a layer of light over the board, `fx.js`, that the pieces
never touch and fingers pass straight through. Each moment has a board
half and a light half, fired together at the instant the piece lands —
the same instant `sound.js` sets it down:

- **A capture is a blow.** The square under it flashes like struck wood
  (the renderer's own light, so the bloom takes it), a shockwave runs
  out across the board, sparks fly, and a few chips of the piece that
  was taken scatter in its own colour and bounce to rest on the board's
  own plane. A pawn is a tap; a queen is a blow. `Fx.powerOf` is the
  one number both halves read, so they always agree. A phone that can
  buzz, buzzes.
- **A check** sends three warm rings out from the king, under the square
  that already breathes.
- **A promotion** raises a pillar of light out of the square, with
  sparkles riding up it — the ceremony `motion.js` began, finished.
- **A mate is the one moment worth all of it.** The mated king is not
  removed or greyed out: he rocks back against the blow, goes over the
  rim of his own base away from whatever mated him, knocks on the board,
  rebounds once and lies there with his crown on the wood — the oldest
  way there is to say a game is over. The camera leans in on him. A
  burst comes out of him and a band of light passes over the whole
  board, the way a lamp carried across a table lights each square in
  turn. Gold when you won it; a soft grey-blue, fewer and slower sparks,
  ash rather than embers when you did not, because nobody should be
  punished with fireworks for losing; a neutral gold when two people at
  one board share it.
- **A new game sets the men out** rank by rank from your side, the middle
  files a beat before the edges, each one dropped onto its square with a
  small hop. The renderer hands back every landing, so the sound can put
  a soft knock under each rank rather than on a timer that hopes, and
  the start motif waits until the last man is still. If the coach plays
  its first move before the last man lands, the rest keep landing round
  it.

**The end card is the other half of a mate.** It waits for the king to
go down before it rises, and on a phone it rises as a sheet from the
bottom over an unblurred board, so the fallen king stays in view. It
leads with the result, the number and what to do next; the longer words
follow the buttons. Then it plays out in order, because the result is
the headline:

1. a win brings confetti and fireworks up with the card;
2. the rating counts up from where it was to where it is, a light tick
   on every step, and lands on a rise or a step home;
3. any new stars land one at a time, each with the ding of a right
   answer and a sparkle;
4. a settled rating, a new title, a new best or an outgrown coach is
   named, with confetti of its own;
5. alongside all of that, the room **looks back over your moves**, with
   a bar that fills while it works — the honest way to show a computer
   thinking. Every move the gentle coach already reviewed during the
   game is reused rather than thought about twice. The look-back gives
   an accuracy, *"you played like about …"* once there are enough moves
   to say it, and your slips counted in the coach's own words:
   inaccuracies, mistakes, blunders, and the `!` and `!!` moves too — or
   *not a single slip*. The third star, if you earned it, lands here.

And it says what to do next: after a win, the next coach up the ladder
is one button away; after a loss to a coach well above you, a step down
is offered, because a step down the ladder is how everyone climbs it.
After *Look back*, a Result chip brings the card back.

Over the whole page, for that card: confetti that behaves like paper —
it tumbles, so you see its shaded back; it sways; and it falls slower
broadside than edge-on, which is the flutter — fireworks, sparkles, a
shake, and a buzz where the phone has one (an iPhone does not, and that
is fine: it is seasoning, never the only way to know).

Three rules keep it in a room this warm:

**It is light, not noise.** Everything is stamped from three little
procedural pictures — a glow, a soft puff, a four-pointed star — painted
once and reused, with no filters and no blur in the frame loop. Four
hundred particles is the ceiling; a celebration and a mate at once are
held to it. A slow device is noticed by its frame times, not guessed
from its name, and quietly given fewer.

**It is asleep when nothing is happening.** The loop runs only while
something is alive; when the last ember goes out the layers are hidden,
shrunk to a single pixel so they hold no memory either, and the loop
stops asking for frames. `fx-check.js` counts the frames it asks for
afterwards, and the answer has to be zero.

**It respects stillness.** Under `prefers-reduced-motion` it draws
nothing at all, the camera does not move, the king is simply lying down,
and a number that would have counted up simply arrives.

Where a square is on screen is the renderer's business: both boards
answer `squareToScreen`, and effects that belong to a square ask again
every frame and are drawn through it — moved with the square and grown
as it comes nearer — so a spark stays on its square, at its size, while
you orbit or the camera leans in. The push-in itself is a layer over
your own view rather than a change to it: orbit and pinch keep working
through it, and easing out returns you to exactly where you were.

Building it found two things worth confessing. On the carved board a
captured piece had always slid away from the piece that took it while
tipping back toward it — a lean axis with the wrong sign; it now falls
the way it is pushed, as the drawn board always drew it. And the mated
king first came to rest at an angle somebody guessed, a little short of
flat — which, for a man turning on the rim of his base, left him
leaning on nothing with his crown in the air. A king's crown is
narrower than his foot, so he really lies a little *past* flat, head
lower than foot. `Motion.lieOf` now measures that off the man's own
shape — the carved board's mesh, the drawn board's outline — so every
set, including one dropped in from outside, lies on the wood, and
`motion-check.js` turns him every way he could fall to prove no part of
him ends up under it. When the board is redrawn under a finished game —
a switch between 2D and 3D, or coming back from looking at an earlier
move — he is laid down again without the fall, exactly where it left
him.

## What the pieces sound like

The room had one oscillator and a switch statement: every piece, in
every material, made the same soft blip. That is the audio equivalent of
drawing all six men as the same cylinder, and the fix is the one
`motion.js` already made for movement.

A piece set down on a board is a **struck resonator** — a short noisy
contact, then a few modes that ring and die away. Which modes, how fast
they die, and how much noise there is at the start, is exactly what your
ear uses to tell glass from wood without being told. So `sound.js`
models that, and three things decide every sound in the room:

**The material** picks the partials and how long they ring. A struck
block of something homogeneous rings at roughly 1 : 2.76 : 5.40 : 8.93 —
the free-bar series — and ivory, porcelain and glass sit on it, which is
why they have a pitch you could sing. Metal does not, which is why a
bell does not. Rendered and measured — timed above 200 Hz, where the
piece itself rings, rather than on the thud through the table that
every material shares — the ring comes out:

| | ink | wood | ivory | porcelain | glass | metal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| rings for | 0.098s | 0.104s | 0.169s | 0.309s | 0.665s | 0.830s |

**The piece** picks how low and how loud. Mass is taken from a real
weighted tournament set rather than from the carved geometry, because
the geometry is whatever set happens to be loaded and the ear has an
expectation older than any of them: a pawn is about a third of a king.
Two similar shapes ring at the cube root of their mass ratio; this is a
shade steeper than that, because half an octave between a pawn and a
king is a difference you hear and a third of one is a difference you
only measure. Shape is the other half — a tall thin piece rings on, a
squat one is duller, and the rook is the squat one.

**The board** is part of it too. A polished board is a harder, brighter
contact than a matte one; a textured one drags noisier. Both come
straight off the Studio's sliders.

### Friction

This is the part you only notice when it is missing. A piece that slides
across the board makes a sound the whole way, and the sound follows how
fast it is going — so a rook shoves and coasts, a king is slow at both
ends, and a queen crossing the whole board is the loudest drag on it.

The amplitude is the piece's own speed curve, gated by how far off the
board it has been lifted, read from the very plan the renderer is
animating. Which means **the knight makes none** — the knight is
carried, not slid, and that falls out of `motion.js` rather than being a
special case here. Measured in the rendered audio, a sliding rook is
thirteen times louder during its travel than a knight is during its hop.

The whole gesture is scheduled in one go off that plan, on the audio
clock: the drag for as long as the piece is on the board, the knock of
whatever it takes going over in its own voice, the rook of a castle in
its own weight at its own moment, and the set-down at the end. The
renderer's animation is measured from the same instant on a clock that
cannot drift apart from it, so the sound of a piece landing is the sound
of that piece landing rather than a timer that hopes.

Everything is synthesized. There are no samples, nothing to download,
and it works on the train.

## The feel

Polished phone games are felt as much as seen, and almost none of that
is buzzing. It is the little detent of a picker wheel, the knock of a
thing set down, a heavier thump when something big is hit — short,
crisp, and at exactly the instant the eye sees it. A buzz on every touch
is noise, and noise is the first thing anyone switches off. So
`haptics.js` is a small vocabulary rather than a rumble, and it is mostly
restraint.

A phone's motor cannot be asked how hard to push, only for how long. Six
to twelve milliseconds reads as a click; twenty or thirty as weight;
longer as a rumble. Every moment is written down as data in those terms:

- **Picking a man up** is one light tick under your finger.
- **Carrying him onto a square he can go to** is the lightest tick there
  is, once per square as he crosses onto it: the detent. You can feel
  your way across the board.
- **Setting him down** is a knock, timed to the landing off the same plan
  the board animates and the synth plays — a pawn a little lighter than
  a queen or a king.
- **A capture** is a real hit, harder the more was taken, at the same
  instant the sparks fly. Take a rook or a queen and the table shakes a
  moment afterwards, dying away.
- **A castle** is two ticks: the rook, then the king, as far apart as the
  board really sets them down.
- **A check** is two sharp beats. **A promotion** rises: three beats, each
  heavier, with the pillar of light. **A mate you gave** builds, beat on
  beat; **a mate you took** is one long, low one; **a draw** is two even,
  soft beats.
- **Letting go where a man cannot go** is a little head shake: two quick,
  soft buzzes, and he goes home.
- **The last five seconds on your clock** beat with the heartbeat you
  hear, lub-dub.
- **The men arriving** at a new game are barely there, one per rank as it
  lands. **The end card** ticks as your rating counts up, pops as each
  star lands, and gives a small fanfare for a rating gained or a level
  outgrown. **A lesson** nods when you get it and gently shakes its head
  when you don't.
- **Buttons** go down the instant a finger lands — a touch smaller, a
  shade darker — and come back up with a small spring past where they
  started, with a light tick on the way down. Only on a press, never on
  hover; only scale and brightness, which move nothing around them; and
  no movement at all under reduced motion.

Your own moves are felt in full; the other side's are felt softly. The
point is to put you in the game, not to jolt you every time the coach
replies. Two people sharing one screen are both "you".

Ticks never smear. Two closer than about a thirtieth of a second run
together into a buzz, so nothing is ever played that close: the floods
(the detents, a number counting) are dropped when they crowd, never
queued, because a queue of stale ticks is worse than a missing one, and
anything that matters waits the few milliseconds it needs. A new game, a
take-back or a hidden tab takes every buzz still waiting with it.

It has its own switch in Settings, separate from sound — plenty of people
play muted and still want to feel the board.

**On an iPhone** there is no `navigator.vibrate` at all. But since Safari
17.4 a checkbox with the `switch` attribute is drawn as a system switch,
and toggling one gives the same small tick the system's own switches do.
So the room keeps one, hidden — off-screen, out of reach of Tab, a
screen reader and a finger, taking no room — and plays a pattern as
ticks at its beats. Strength cannot be varied that way, so a heavier
moment gets an extra tick or two instead. It is a trick rather than an
API, and Apple may change it; if it stops working the room loses a
little seasoning and nothing else, because every moment here is also
seen and, if you like, heard. A device with nothing to buzz (a laptop, a
desktop) says so under the switch, and everything else still works.

`haptics-check.js` plays the whole vocabulary in node against a fake
motor and a fake clock: every pattern sane, the throttle dropping and
holding as it should, off really off, and one tick per beat on the
iPhone path.

## The keys

Every button in the room is a key cut from the same block. Its face is
lit from above and stands a few pixels proud of a darker edge; press it
and the face travels down onto the edge, a little light falls where it
was touched, and on release it comes back up on a spring, a hair past
where it started. Nothing around a key moves when it does: only
transform, shadow and brightness change.

There are three depths, so the eye can rank a card without reading it.
Small keys (the chips, the action bar) stand three pixels up; the rows
and tiles you choose between stand four; and the one hero on a card,
*Play the computer*, or the rematch when a game ends, stands six, in
gold, with a slow light crossing it now and then. Gold always means the
same thing: this is the way forward, or this is what is chosen. A key
that cannot be pressed sits down in its bed, dim. The quiet way out of a
toast stays flat, because it is the way out.

The on/off settings are drawn as what they are, switches, with a thumb
that slides and stretches a little under the finger. The word "On" or
"Off" stays in the page for a screen reader, with `role="switch"` and
its state kept true by `press.js`.

A press is made of moments too short to style, so `press.js` keeps them:

- **A quick tap is still seen.** A pressed key stays down at least 110 ms,
  so the fastest tap shows the whole journey down and back.
- **A scroll is not a press.** On glass a key waits 70 ms before going
  down; if the browser takes the touch to scroll, or the finger drifts,
  it never does. A tap shorter than the wait presses as it ends.
- **The key knows where it was touched**, so the light is under the
  finger and not in the middle.
- **Enter and Space** press a key with the same travel.

Each press has a small dry sound whose pitch says what kind of key it
was (a plain key, a gold one, the hero, a tab, a quiet link), a switch
has one voice for on and a lower one for off, and the thumb gets a tick
where the device can give one. A card's keys arrive a beat apart, top to
bottom. With reduced motion a press is still marked, by the edge going
and the face darkening, and nothing moves or arrives.

Without `press.js` every key still presses on `:active`.
`press-check.js` checks the moments in node and the look in a browser.

## The room's music

The pieces were modelled; everything else was a sine beep. A beep can
tell you *that* something happened. It cannot tell you how to feel about
it, and that is the whole job of a signal in a game. The same two notes
that say "check!" when you give it should sound like "careful" when it is
given to you. Losing should sound like a friend's hand on your shoulder,
not a game-show buzzer.

So every moment that is not a piece touching wood is now a short piece
of music, written down as data the way `motion.js` writes down a
gesture: which notes, when, how hard, on which instrument. A small synth
plays it, with four pitched voices and three unpitched ones:

- a **marimba**, from the board's own family, for the motifs;
- a **felt piano** for the harmony, its partials stretched a hair sharp
  the way real strings are;
- an **FM chime** (two operators at 1 : 3.5, the classic tubular bell)
  for every reward. The modulation dies within a quarter of a second,
  so it strikes bright and rings pure;
- a **pad** of two triangle waves a few cents apart, one in each ear.
  The beating between them is the shimmer, and the split is the stereo
  width;
- a **thump** that drops in pitch as it lands, plus air for a card
  opening and a tick for the clock.

**One key for the whole room: D major.** Every moment agrees with every
other one, so a check that lands while the start motif is still ringing
makes a chord rather than a collision, and the endings have a home note
to come back to. D also puts the chimes where a phone speaker is at its
best. The one note outside the key is deliberate: the check you are in
borrows a B-flat from the minor, so it reads as "careful" rather than
"wrong".

The moments that matter most were written with the most care. **Mate**
is a real fanfare: three quick notes on the dominant (the way fanfares
have started since the hunting horn), then I, IV and V, then the home
chord spread across four octaves with a low weight under it and bells
over it. **Being mated** is B minor, then G with its seventh, then D.
The minor chord admits it, the next one softens it, and the last one is
home, because the next game starts from home. **A wrong answer in a
lesson** is a low open fifth, neither major nor minor, so it carries no
verdict at all: getting things wrong is how a lesson works. **A
promotion** gives each piece its own figure; the knight's arpeggio
jumps, steps sideways and jumps again. **The last seconds on a clock**
are a heartbeat rather than an alarm, and its second beat comes sooner
as the time runs down. **A rating counting up** on the end card climbs
the pentatonic as it goes, at most twenty ticks a second, because
clicks any closer than that stop being clicks and become a buzz. Each
star lands with the lesson's right-answer ding; a milestone gets a
peal, and outgrowing a coach a run of chimes. A game you start begins
with a short rising motif once the last man has been set down — never
at boot, where a resumed game would leave it waiting for the first tap.
A queen or rook coming off the board lands with weight under it.

**The room itself is grown, not recorded.** A real room's echo is, to a
very good approximation, noise that dies away exponentially and loses
its top end first. So that is what is generated, once per audio context,
from a seeded generator: 2.4 seconds of stereo, with a different seed
for each ear, which is what makes it wide. It is grown at an idle moment
after your first tap, so the first fanfare does not pay for it.

Kindness has some mechanics to it too:

- **Off is off.** A muted room renders silence. A mute fades a fanfare
  that is already playing, and the room's echo of it, rather than
  letting it finish over somebody who just asked for quiet. The room
  tone — the place's own quiet, under *Where you play* — can be
  switched off in the Studio; switched off, it lets go of every node,
  and it never hums in a tab nobody is looking at.
- **A sound belongs to its moment.** A browser will not start the audio
  clock until somebody touches the page, and anything asked of a
  stopped clock is saved up for the instant it starts. So anything
  asked for before then — a sting, or a piece being set down — waits
  only as long as a tap takes to start the clock, and is let go after
  that. A finished game restored at boot does not greet your first tap
  with its fanfare, and a coach's reply made at boot does not set down
  a piece on it that nobody moved.
- **Less motion** keeps the harmony of every moment and drops the
  sparkles and the sweeps.
- **A busy room** gives up only as much as it has to: first the
  sparkles, then the colour of the opening notes, and only then whole
  notes — always from the beginning, because the end is the part
  anybody remembers. A mate that lands on top of a promotion that took
  a queen still gets its whole last chord.
- **Phones** were listened to as well. The heartbeat and the weight
  under a big capture live mostly below 200 Hz, which is exactly where a
  phone speaker gives up, so each carries a soft knock in the mid-range:
  heard there, and not only on headphones.

Every voice is planned as data before anything is built. That lets
`sound-check.js` prove in node that each note starts and ends at
silence inside the length its sting promises, and what a busy room
would keep. It then hears it all again in a browser, including on a
page whose audio clock waits for a real tap, the way a phone's does.

## The coach's notebook

Everything the room noticed used to live for seven seconds in a toast
and then be gone. That is no use to a beginner, who wanted to read it
twice and could not, and no use to a strong player, who wanted the whole
game at once and got a sentence at a time.

So it is kept, beside the moves, on its own tab. Openings, tactics
spotted by `teach.js`, what a move cost, how the game ended — each entry
remembers which move it belongs to, so tapping it takes the board back
to that position and draws the squares the remark is about. Where a
remark is about a danger, the arrow points the way the danger travels:
from the piece about to take to the piece about to be taken, and from
the square a rook would land on to the king it would mate. A take-back
forgets what was said about the moves it undid; a new game starts clean.

The same observation comes in two languages, chosen in Settings:

- **Plain words** — full sentences with the idea named, because naming
  is what turns a thing that happened into a pattern you can recognise
  again.
- **Notation** — one line, the way a club player already reads it:
  `? Kxf7! · Nc3 was better by 1.1`, `C55 · Two Knights Defence`,
  `skewer · f3–f7–f8`.
- **Off** — the room says nothing and marks nothing. What it noticed
  earlier is kept and comes straight back when the voice does.

A mark rides along to the move list — `?!`, `?`, `??`, `!`, `!!` —
because that one piece of notation means the same thing on your fifth
game and your five thousandth. The plain words behind it are a hover
or a tap away.

The marks come from the same like-for-like comparison the gentle coach
already ran: the move you played and the move the engine wanted, scored
by the same yardstick, subtracted. **Praise needed a second question.**
"How much worse than the best was this?" cannot tell a found move from a
forced one, so `review` now also measures how far clear of the
*next-best* move the played one stood — one more entry in a list that
was already being scored. And praise is withheld where it would teach
the wrong lesson: not while you were in check (getting out of it is not
a choice), not taking back on the square they just took on, not when
there was barely anything else legal, and not in the opening, where the
good move is usually something somebody told you rather than something
you found. Across a few hundred engine-played moves that comes out at
roughly one mark in eight — the coach noticing, rather than the coach
chattering.

The board is only interrupted for one thing now: a blunder you can still
take back, said while the position is still in front of you. Everything
quieter is in the notebook, where it can be read twice.

## The help lamp

💡 used to sell one thing: a move. A coach across a real table asks
*"what does that threaten?"* far more often than they name a move, and
strong players differ from weak ones mostly in that habit — the rules
pages teach it (checks, captures, threats) and then nothing helped you
practise it. So the button now says **Help**, and tapping it offers
three things at once rather than selling one:

- **🧭 Think** — the questions, built from the position on the board:
  *"You have two checks and one capture to look at."* · *"Their last move
  was Nc3 — what does it attack?"* · one question about the position
  (your quietest piece, a king still in the middle, a piece that hasn't
  moved, a king that should be working in an ending). The counts are the
  rules' own. Loose pieces are counted, not located — *"one piece of
  yours is loose. Which?"* — unless you are behind, when the room was
  already giving that word away for free. Free; kept in Notes.
- **👁 Their idea** — what their last move did, in true words, with its
  line on the board: the book's own reason when the move is in a curated
  line, otherwise the teacher's sentence turned round to your side of
  the table (*"unless you stop it"*, not *"unless they stop it"*). Free;
  kept in Notes.
- **💡 A move** — the hint that was always there, at the price it always
  had (the chip shows it). This is the only chip that costs anything,
  and the only help that makes a rated game count for less.

**Fair about threats.** Naming a threat against you is a hint in all but
name, and hints are priced by the odds. So when you are level or ahead,
a sentence that would name one of your pieces that can really be won is
held back, and the explanation ends with a question instead — *"Is
everything of yours still guarded?"* — with the price of the move chip
beside it. Nobody is charged for anything they didn't press. **A mate
threat is the one exception: always named, never charged.** The room
already draws the mate net for nothing on every check, and a coach who
answered "is everything guarded?" while you walked into mate would be
being cute, not kind.

**A why for every coach move.** The search that chose the coach's move
also knew what it expected next; that used to be thrown away. Now, when
the coach's move has something true to say about it — a capture, a
threat, a developing move, the book's reason — one quiet line goes into
the notebook, and tapping the last move in the move list asks the same
question out loud. Looking back at any earlier move, the banner offers
**❓ Why this move?**, for your moves as well as theirs (and **?** does it
from the keyboard). Once you have answered a move, its why can also say
what the coach was counting on — *"The coach was counting on …Nf6 d3"* —
but not before: that line contains the engine's guess at *your* best
reply, which is a hint nobody paid for. The line is kept in memory for
the game only; after a reload the why simply leaves that clause out.
When the teacher has nothing it can prove about a move, the why says so
and asks what it might prepare. The old rule-of-thumb reasons a hint
used to fall back on (*"rooks love open files"*) are gone for the same
reason.

The notation voice wants the move, not a menu: there, 💡 goes straight
to it. `node chess/tools/help-check.js` holds the words to the teacher's
bar without a browser; `lamp-check.js` checks the till in a real one.

## The first five minutes

A newcomer used to meet a rating card that said *Not rated yet* and
twelve tiles, with *New to chess?* on the third row. Now a first visit
— no games, no lessons solved, no game waiting — finds one question
where the rating card will be: **do you know how the pieces move?**

- **🐣 I'm new to chess** opens the twelve rules pages, whose last
  button already starts a game against the gentlest coach — with the
  training wheels on, said once when they first show.
- **▶ I know how the pieces move** goes straight into a game against
  the coach the ladder suggests.

Under them, one line on why playing alone here is worth it: every game
against the computer moves a number that's yours, the coach says what
it sees, and the Academy makes it stick. There is no tour, no arrows
pointing at buttons and no mascot. During the first two games against
the computer the room says four things, each once, at the moment it
helps: that the coach keeps what it notices in **Notes** (the first time
it writes one), that **help is free when you're behind** (the first
time you're ahead), that **a take-back makes the game count for a
little less** (inside the first blunder toast, which is the moment it
is offered), and that **you can tap the coach's move to ask why** (its
first move). Four is the ceiling; a fifth would be a tutorial. They are
remembered in your preferences, so a reload does not repeat them.

The welcome is retired by the thing it asked for — a finished game
against the computer — never by a timer, and the lobby shows your first
number instead. `node chess/tools/welcome-check.js` plays that on a
phone and counts what was said.

## Training wheels

Hanging pieces decide most games under a thousand, and the first twenty
games are mostly a sequence of dropped pieces — which is where most new
players quit. **Training wheels** (Settings; on for the *I'm new* door;
offered once from the first blunder toast as *🛟 Warn me first*) put a
hand on the saddle:

- At the start of your turn, your pieces that **can really be won**
  breathe in the warning colour — at most three, worth a minor piece or
  more — and the attacker's line is drawn to the most valuable one. A
  mate threat against you is drawn from the piece that would deliver it.
  One line goes in the notebook; nothing is said aloud.
- Lift a piece and the squares where it **would be lost** ring in the
  warning colour instead of showing a dot: a capture that loses the
  exchange, or a quiet move to a square where the piece can be won. It
  is a wheel, not a wall — a hot square still takes the move, and the
  gentle review then does its job.

Every mark rests on the teacher's own standard: a piece "can be won"
only when the exchange count wins it *and* the win survives the capture
being played out, the same test that stops the coach crying wolf. The
breath lasts a few seconds and then holds still, so the board (and the
phone) can rest. The hot squares are bounded — a lifted piece looks at
no more than thirty destinations, and on a phone too slow to do it
quickly they drop for the session rather than slowing every tap.

Against the computer the wheels are help, and counted as such: a rated
game in which they warned you counts as **75% of a game** — once, not
per warning — and cannot earn the clean star. The end card says so,
and the record keeps it. Two people sharing a board pay nothing for a
shared lesson. They never show with a clock running (a warning that
costs time is unfair) or against a friend on another device. And after
a full game in which nothing of yours was ever in danger, the room asks
once whether to try the next one without them.
`node chess/tools/wheels-check.js` checks the marks against the
exchange count on both boards.

## The coach

A teaching engine has a different job from a strong one. It still has to
be right — advice you can't trust is worse than no advice — but the
number it produces has to mean something a person can act on.

**Strength, because advice rests on it.** The search was a plain
alpha-beta that reached depth five or six in a second, which is enough to
take a hanging queen and not enough to see why it was hanging. It now
carries a transposition table on a Zobrist key, killer and history move
ordering, null-move pruning, late-move reductions, check extensions and
aspiration windows, over a tapered evaluation that knows about passed and
isolated pawns, mobility, open files, the bishop pair and the shelter
round the king. Same second, depth eight to eleven, on a third of the
nodes. Played against the engine it replaced, 24 games at 150ms a move:
**+12 =12 −0, about +338 Elo.**

**The quiet word, rebuilt.** The old blunder check asked "is the
opponent's best reply good for them?" — which flags every move made in a
bad position and says nothing when you throw away a winning one. The
question that teaches is *how much worse was that than the best you had*,
so `Chess.review()` runs on the position before the move and scores the
move you played and the move the engine wanted **with the same call at
the same depth**. Mixing a deep score with a shallow one is the subtle
way this feature goes wrong, and it is why the answer used to be a shrug.

The difference has a name players already use — inaccuracy, mistake,
blunder — and using their words means the feedback transfers to every
book and every board afterwards. Then it names the punishment before the
cure, because *"Qxe5+ is the problem"* is what you want to spot next
time; *"Qe7 was the move"* is only what to memorise.

**Off the drawing thread.** A move from the strongest coach used to hold
the main thread for most of a second: the clock stopped, the board
froze, taps went nowhere. The work was never the problem — doing it
where the page draws was. `brain.js` moves it to a worker and the worst
frame gap during a full-second search is now zero. That is also what makes it safe to let
the coach think for longer. And when the board changes underneath a
search — a new game, a take-back, a story or a lesson begun —
`Brain.cancel()` ends it, rather than letting the next question wait
behind an answer nobody will read.

**A ladder you can practise against.** A level used to stop on the
clock, which meant it was simply stronger on a faster laptop. Now every
level is a budget of positions as well as plies, so it plays the same
game on a slow phone as on a fast laptop — a coach whose strength
depends on your hardware can't be trained against, and can't carry a
rating. It also starts every move from an empty memory. The engine keeps
one table of positions it has already read, shared by the coach, the
hints and the reviews, and a level that reads two plies, replying just
after a hint that read six, used to choose with six plies of knowledge:
along one test game, a review run just before the reply changed between
a quarter and two-thirds of the weaker levels' moves. Asking for a hint
made the coach's next move sharper. Emptying the table first costs a
fraction of a millisecond and changes nothing else. Below the top, the
opponent plays under its strength on purpose, weighted toward the better
moves rather than picking evenly from everything it can get away with:
mostly sensible, occasionally loose, which is what a real player of that
level looks like. There are eight levels now, each with a measured
rating (see *The score*), and the reviews run at the depth the accuracy
table was measured at, so the gentle coach's reviews and the end card's
look-back are one and the same. `engine-check.js --slow` plays every
level against its neighbour to confirm the ladder is still a ladder;
`calibrate.js` measures how far apart the rungs are.

## The teacher has to be right

A name is the most useful thing a beginner can be given, and the most
dangerous. "That's a skewer" turns a thing that happened into a pattern
you'll look for again, so a wrong name is worse than silence: you learn
the wrong pattern with total confidence. The coach used to call 3.Bb5 in
the Ruy López a skewer, 3.Bc4 a pin, and 2.Qh5 a pin of the h-pawn. Once,
in the Fried Liver, it praised 8.Qf3+ as a skewer whose follow-up, Qxf8,
loses the queen to the rook on h8. All of it came from comparing piece
values on a line and never asking whether anything could actually be
won.

So underneath every remark is now a **static exchange count**: play out
every capture on a square, cheapest piece first, each side free to stop
when going on would lose. Pinned pieces sit it out, a rook behind a rook
joins in by itself, and a pawn that reaches the last rank counts as what
it becomes. On top of it:

- A **fork** has to win something whatever they reply, and "and then the
  rook falls" is only said when the rook falls after every reply.
- A **pin** has to stop the front piece doing something it wanted to
  do; the piece behind has to be genuinely winnable, and the pinning
  piece mustn't simply be lost itself. A pin you *walk into* is the
  cheapest kind to avoid, so the coach now warns about that too — and
  never scolds a block of a check, because that pin was forced.
- A **skewer** needs the front piece genuinely attacked and has to
  survive every reply, including the king stepping over to guard what's
  behind it.
- The **back rank** used to mean "the pawns in front of the king haven't
  moved", which is every king in the starting position. Now the king
  must really have no square off its back row; a rook is set down on a
  copy of the board, the rules are asked whether it would be mate, and
  the square is named.
- **"That threatens mate"** is said only when the move made the threat,
  and said first — a quiet 3.Qh5 against f7 is about the mate, not about
  f7 being pinned.

One square is not the whole board, though. "Careful — that knight hangs"
about a knight that is bait teaches the opposite of the truth: take it,
and the rook it was guarding falls instead. So before the coach calls
anything **hanging**, it plays the capture out once and counts the
owner's best answer: a mate, or a capture elsewhere, plus whatever the
move itself just took. A piece that is won back like that was never
lost. A capture that does lose its piece is told as one ("you've given a
knight for a pawn"), never as a piece "taken for free". When the honest
answer is "that's just a trade", the coach says nothing.

The same eyes now **explain hints**. Ask for a hint and
`Teach.explainMove` names what the move really does: it wins the knight,
completes an exchange, forks the king and rook, threatens mate, pins the
knight to the queen, steps the king out of check, blocks the check. If it
gives up material on one square to take more on another, it names the
second capture too: "if they take back, Nxc7 takes their queen". If none
of that is true, it returns nothing, and the room falls back on a
principle rather than dressing one up as a reason. A forced mate is
still said first.

The **opening stories** explain every move now — all 37 lines, 141
moves. A story that explains half its moves teaches that the other half
are magic. Reasons live in one table keyed by the moves that lead to
them, so a move shared by several lines is explained the same way in
all of them. Any reason that claims something checkable — an attack, a
defence, a pin, a pawn offered or won back, a mate next move — is
checked on the board by `book-check.js` after that very move. Writing
them turned up wrong chess that had shipped: the Scholar's Mate line
claimed 2.Qh5 threatens Qxf7#, which it doesn't, because the king takes
back. And a story used to flash each reason in a toast for under three
seconds, with no way to stop it. Now it has a bar of its own — pause,
step, go back — and a reason stays on screen as long as it takes to
read, and stays in the notebook after. At the end, *Play on from here*
turns the position into a game.

The **Academy** gets the engine as a second marker. A lesson once taught
a losing promotion with a confident explanation, so `lesson-check.js`
now asks, for every lesson, whether each accepted answer actually holds
and whether each "wrong" answer really is worse. Five more lessons
failed that test and were rebuilt. Honesty runs the schedule too: a
lucky guess counts as not knowing it yet and comes back tomorrow, a
half-guess halves the interval, and solving the same idea three times
in ten minutes is practice, not three spaced reviews. Saved progress is
rebuilt field by field when it is read, so a damaged save cannot stop
the Academy opening — even in a private window where storage throws on
every read. *New to chess?* ends by starting a first game against the
gentlest coach, rather than dropping you back at the menu, and the end
card's *Practise what this game showed you* picks the most important
idea the game turned on and, of those, the one you know least.

Finally, the checks were checked. Each guard in `teach.js` was broken on
purpose, one at a time, to see that something noticed. Where nothing
did, a position was found that needs the guard, and it is now a case.
And `teach-check.js` reads every legal move at every fourth position of
a few random games — thousands of calls — and none may throw, print
"undefined", or point an arrow off the board.

## Lines, and your own pen

Every line on the board is now one line. The hint, a free nudge, the
coach naming a fork, a note you tap an hour later, a lesson's answer,
the look-back's better move, your own pen: all of them are the same
ribbon from `lines.js`, drawn on from the piece to the square in a
little over a third of a second, because the direction *is* the
content and drawing it on shows the direction before your eye has to
work it out. One easing, one duration, no bounce; ask for less motion
and every line is simply there. Colour is not a number any more but a
meaning — a hint, a threat, a lane, your plan — and each board turns the
meaning into its own skin's colour, so a threat on the walnut board and
a threat on the glass one are the same threat. Lines live in layers,
each on its own timer, so a nudge arriving while a hint is up no longer
wipes the hint. And a line only keeps the board drawing while it is
arriving or carries a spark: a still one lets the board sleep.

**Your own pen.** Hold a square and drag on a phone — or right-drag with
a mouse — and a ribbon grows under your finger, snapping to each square
it reaches with a small detent. Let go where you started and it is a
ring instead. Draw the same shape again to rub it out; tap an empty
square with nothing in your hand, press Escape, or use the little ✎
pill at the top of the board to clear the lot. There is no toolbar and
no colour to pick, because the board already knows what you meant: a
stroke that starts on one of *their* men is about what that man
threatens, drawn in the capture colour; anything else is your plan, in
the skin's own plan colour (every look has one; a shared look that never
carried one takes its preset's). On a desk, Shift and Alt say it
outright. The 3D board still turns on a left-drag from an empty square,
and on a finger that moves before it has been held.

Your drawing belongs to the **position**, not the screen. Play on and
the live board is clean; step back and it is exactly where you left it;
reload and it is still there; take a move back and the drawing on the
position that no longer exists goes with it. That is the difference
between a note on move 12 and graffiti on move 20, and it is why
looking back — the room's main way of learning — gets better rather
than messier when you draw. It is a note, not an event, so it never
asks for a frame: a board with your arrows on it sleeps like any other.
If you like to rest a finger on a piece while you think, Settings has a
switch for the held-finger pen; the right button always draws.

**The line behind the words.** Every sentence the coach says that names
a move can now be seen. "**Qxe5+** is the problem" is drawn as a threat
while the take-back offer is on the screen — but only while the board
still shows the position right after your move, because that is the
only position it is true of. **👀 Show me** goes back to the position
before the slip: your move as a faint ghost, the better one drawn as the
hint, the coach's remark in the banner; → steps to the problem drawn on
the position after; → again is the game. The end card's "Look back"
lands on the first move worth a second look, with ◀ ▶ in the banner to
walk from one to the next, and tapping a marked move or its note brings
the same lines with it. A hint from the engine's own search offers
**Show the line**, and the next three plies grow on a beat apart — theirs
coming at you, yours as a ghost — then go. No line ever starts on an
empty square, at most three are drawn at once, and nothing is drawn
that the room has not already said in words. Two honest limits: the
refutation exists only for a slip that cost a pawn or more, so an
inaccuracy has no threat drawn; and a game the coach reviewed before
this (an older save) shows its lines only once it is looked over again.
A clocked game gets no gentle review while it is played, so its lines
come from the end card's look-back as that works through your moves.

`tools/lines-check.js` holds the grammar to account in Node;
`tools/ink-check.js` draws with a real mouse and a real finger;
`tools/lookback-check.js` plays a real blunder and checks every line
lands on the square it names.

## Keys, and a board that speaks

The room can now be played without a mouse, and without seeing it.

**Focus that behaves.** While a card is open, the room behind it is
inert: Tab stays in the card, and a screen reader cannot wander into
the game underneath. Opening a card puts focus in it — on its first
button when it asks a question or offers the promotion picker, on its
heading otherwise — and closing the last one puts focus back where it
was. It is one door for every card, an observer on the cards' own
`hide` class, so none of the two dozen places that open a card had to
change. The Studio, closed, is out of reach too (it used to leave its
twenty inputs in the tab order). An amber ring marks focus, for a
keyboard only: a tap or a click never shows it.

**The board speaks.** Each board is a named control ("Chess board.
Arrow keys move around it…"), and one polite live region reads the game:
each move as a sentence — "White: knight to f3, check", "Black: pawn
from e5 takes knight on d4", "castles kingside", "becomes a queen" —
with "Your move" when it is; the result; a toast that asks something;
and whatever answers a key you just pressed. Settings → *Spoken board*:
**Moves** (the default — a screen-reader user hears the game without
hunting for a switch, and nobody else hears anything, because the
region is invisible), **Everything** (every remark of the coach's too),
or **Off**. Every move in the list carries its sentence as its name,
and the current move is the list's one stop in the tab order. Shift W
reads where everything is: whose move, then each side's pieces,
strongest first, and the last move.

**The board under the keys.** Tab to the board and a cursor appears on
your king, drawn by both the carved and the flat board, and the square
is read out ("e1, white king"). The arrows walk it, turned the right way
when the board is; Enter or Space picks up and sets down, with the same
sound and feel as a tap and a word on where the piece can go; Escape
puts it back; `[` and `]` step back and forward through the game. A
click on the board never takes focus, so for a mouse ← → still look
back through the game exactly as before. A still cursor costs no
frames.

**Type a move.** Start typing anywhere outside a text box and a small
entry opens over the board with your first letter in it: `Nf3`, `e4`,
`exd5`, `O-O`, `e8=Q`, also `nf3`, `ed5`, `0-0`, `e8Q`, or the two
squares, `e2e4` / `e7e8q`. Enter plays it through the same door as two
taps, so lessons, the promotion picker and a friend's board see nothing
new. When it cannot be played the entry stays and says why — "Nbd2 or
Nfd2 — which one?", "No knight of yours can go to f3.", "You can't
castle kingside right now." — and Escape closes it. A pawn sent to the
last rank with no piece named opens the picker, which answers to Q, N,
R and B.

**One key for a button.** Shift and a letter that is not a piece:
**U** take back, **H** help, **F** flip, **L** the last move again,
**M** the menu, **W** where everything is. Plain letters belong to
notation (N and R are pieces, a to h are files), `?` to the lamp's "why
that move", Escape to the cards and the pen, so none of these collides.
**/** shows the keys; so does Settings → *Spoken board* → *The keys*.

Two honest limits. Nothing is ever spoken through the speakers — the
words go to your own screen reader, at your own rate; and a typed move
is read only when you press Enter, so there is no guessing ahead as you
type.

`tools/access-check.js` holds the reader to every legal move in a few
hundred positions in Node, then plays on both boards in a real browser
with the keyboard alone.

## Two ideas worth explaining

**The racing line.** A racing driver's line is one curve that contains a
whole paragraph — brake here, turn in there, that's the apex, everything
after it is exit speed. Nobody reads that mid-corner; they see the line.
Chess advice has the same problem ("your bishop, along this diagonal, is
why their knight can't move") and the same answer. So the board draws a
small grammar of curves, and the meaning lives in the shape: a **run**
tapers *away* from your piece toward where the idea lands; a **threat**
is drawn coming *at* you, so a plan and a danger differ at a glance; a
**lane** is the long straight road of a passed pawn; a **net** is the
ring of squares a mated king can't reach — marks rather than a curve,
because a net is a place, not a direction. Every curve carries a spark
that travels it, because motion is the cheapest teaching there is: you
can't help but follow it. The geometry lives in `lines.js` and is shared,
so a line means exactly the same thing in 2D and in 3D.

**Odds, the old way.** Before rating systems, a stronger player gave
*odds* — a knight, a rook, time on the clock — so the game stayed a real
game. This room does the same thing with help instead of material. Ask
for a hint and the price depends on the position, not on who you are:
**free while you're behind**, a small price when it's level, and steeper
the further ahead you get (paid in clock seconds if you're using a
clock, in marks if you aren't). The hint button wears its own price, so
you always see the trade before you take it, and the price is paid when
the hint arrives, once per position — not again on a second tap. In a
rated game the score notices too, and counts that game for a little
less; the hint itself is never refused. The player who's behind also
gets occasional *free* nudges, unasked — and the player who's ahead
never does, because they don't need them. The end card shows the ledger
("odds given"), names what the game was actually about, and offers the
matching lesson, so a loss turns into the next thing you learn.

## Your clock, your rules

**Any time control.** The new-game card's clock chips are the six usual
presets and *Other…*: whole minutes, 1 to 180, and whole seconds added
back after every move, 0 to 60. The card says what it will start in
words ("12 minutes each, plus 4 seconds back after every move"), a number
past the bounds is held to them as it is typed, and the clock you chose
is remembered for that kind of game, custom or not. The presets are
written once (`CLK_PRESETS` in app.js; the chips are drawn from it), and
every clock that arrives from anywhere — a chip, the two fields, a save,
a remembered preference, the other device's handshake — passes one rule,
`clkOk`, before it is used. Time odds are not offered yet.

**Time that really passed.** The clock is charged by the wall clock:
whatever time went by since it was last read. It used to add up the gaps
between frames and drop any gap over three seconds, so a page the
browser stopped drawing stopped costing time. Pauses are now said rather
than guessed:

- Any card over a game on this device stops its clock — the lobby,
  settings, your record, and now *A friend nearby* too (it used to tick
  on behind it). One list, `CARDS`, says which cards pause.
- Out of sight in a game on this device (the coach, or two people on one
  screen) is a pause, like the menu: the time up to the moment the tab
  went is charged, the time it was gone is not, and the room says "The
  clock waited while you were away."
- Between two devices it is thinking time — the other person waited
  through it — so it is charged in full the moment you come back, and a
  flag that fell meanwhile falls then, before you can move. The charge
  runs from the last time the clock was read, not from when the tab was
  hidden: a move that arrived while you were away resynced both clocks
  and restarted yours from that moment, and only the time since is yours
  to pay.

`node chess/tools/edges-check.js` holds the page's main thread for four
seconds and checks the four seconds were charged, hides the page in a
coach game and in a two-device game, and runs a flag down while away.

## Always a queen

Settings → *Promotion*: *Ask* (the default) or *Always a queen*. Asking
is the default on purpose — the picker is there for the rare
underpromotion, and a convenience should not take that away unasked.
With *Always a queen*, a pawn reaching the last rank becomes a queen in
the same gesture, with the usual sound and flash. The room still asks
when the board can prove the queen is the wrong answer — when a knight,
rook or bishop would mate and the queen wouldn't ("A knight here is
checkmate — the queen isn't."), or when the queen would stalemate and
another piece wouldn't. Lessons always ask, because an Academy answer
can be an underpromotion. In a game between two devices it is your
device's setting; the move travels as it always did.
`node chess/tools/edges-check.js`

## When something breaks

Three errors inside eight seconds bring up the recovery card instead of
a white screen. A promise that fails with nobody listening counts too —
when it is the kind of failure a bug makes (a `TypeError`, a
`ReferenceError`…); a network that is down, a permission refused or a
share sheet dismissed is not the room breaking, and never raises it.

The card says what happened in plain words, and where when it can tell
("…while drawing the 3D board"). *The technical line* opens the message,
file and line, 2D or 3D, the kind of game and how many moves in, with a
button that copies it for a bug report; the last five errors are kept
for this tab only, so nothing about a crash outlives the tab unless you
copy it. Then the choices, each saying what it costs:

- **Try again** — reloads the room. Almost always enough.
- **Try again in 2D** — if the 3D board is the trouble.
- **Keep my record, clear the game in progress** — forgets only the
  unfinished game (on this device or between two, and the four letters
  it was reached by). Your rating, stars, lessons, journal, friends,
  skins and settings stay.
- **Erase everything on this device…** — asks first, naming what goes
  (your rating and its history — "a rating of 1043 from 7 games" — your
  ladder stars, Academy lessons, the journal, feats, friends, saved
  skins, settings and any game in progress), and only then erases it.

The old card's single *Fresh start* did that last thing under a label
that said "Clears the saved game and settings". And a browser that will
not keep what the room saves — a private window, a full disk — is told
so once, rather than discovered after a reload.
`node chess/tools/edges-check.js`

## Why it's honest

- The rules engine is **perft-verified**: its move counts match the
  published node counts for the standard test positions (millions of
  positions deep), so castling-through-check, en-passant pins, and
  underpromotions are provably right. `node chess/tools/perft.js --deep`
- Every opening line in the book is **replayed through the engine** at
  test time; a typo cannot ship — and every reason given for a move that
  claims something on the board is checked on the board.
  `node chess/tools/book-check.js`
- **The help lamp never names a move you did not pay for, and a mate
  threat is always named and never charged.** Its other words are the
  rules' counts or something the teacher has proved; a threat against
  you that would change the game is asked about, not handed over,
  unless you are behind. `node chess/tools/help-check.js`
- **The ladder is measured, not named.** Every coach's rating comes out
  of matches against its neighbours, played with the clock frozen and
  the dice seeded so any game can be replayed. The one assumption — how
  self-play gaps scale to people — is written down beside the numbers.
  `node chess/tools/calibrate.js`, and `rating-check.js` for the
  arithmetic.
- **Joining is four letters.** One of you taps *Invite a friend* and reads
  out something like `BUZZ` (or sends it as a link); the other types it. That's the whole
  ritual, and it is the same one at the domino table and in HIVEMIND —
  one front door across every game here. Boards waiting nearby also
  show up in a list, so you can tap instead of type.
- Play is still **serverless where it counts**: your moves travel
  directly between the two devices (STUN only for candidate discovery).
  The four letters buy nothing but a place to leave a handshake — a
  WebRTC handshake is ~600 characters and can never be read aloud,
  while four letters can. Nothing about the game passes through it — not
  the moves, not the score between friends, not the quiet id that keeps
  two Sams apart, which crosses only the direct link. A game you send is
  a link you hand to someone yourself; it never touches the mailbox.
- **Everything underneath still works.** Offline, on `file://`, or on a
  fork of this without the mailbox, the room falls back by itself to
  the handshake it always had: two phones held up to each other, each
  showing a framed code and watching through its camera at the same
  time. Every code the app sees — scanned, pasted, arriving on the
  clipboard, typed, or tapped as a link — enters through one door
  (`feedCode`) and advances the handshake by itself.
- **The link looks after itself.** A heartbeat rides the same channel
  the moves do, so the chip above the board can tell you the round trip
  in milliseconds — and tell you the difference between your opponent
  thinking and your opponent being in a tunnel. If the link dies, both
  sides quietly find each other again *under the same four letters*
  (the host holds a key that reclaims them), the host re-states the
  whole game in one frame, and the clocks stop while nobody can move.
  Nobody reads out a new code, because there isn't one.
- **A flat battery is an inconvenience, not a loss.** The code, your
  seat and the game so far are written down after every move; the join
  screen offers *Rejoin your board* for eight hours afterwards.
- **One release at a time.** A page and its scripts are one program
  written in two files, and they only work as the pair they shipped as.
  The service worker used to fetch the page fresh while answering the
  scripts from the old cache, so for one visit after every release the
  room was new buttons wired to old code. Now both come from the same
  versioned cache: a release downloads whole beside the old one, the
  update bar offers it, and one tap swaps everything at once. The price
  is that a deploy reaches you one tap later. The payoff is that the
  room is never half of two versions, and it opens without waiting on
  the network. The old worker still gets to hand out one last mixed
  visit before it is replaced — nothing new can stop that — so the
  release that replaces it doesn't wait for a tap: it takes over as soon
  as it has downloaded, and the room reloads itself, whole. A room left
  open for days asks for a new release whenever you come back to the
  tab. `offline-check.js` ships both kinds of release in a real browser
  to prove it.
- **Self-healing** by design: saves are written after every move and
  survive reloads; a corrupt save is quietly retired, and so is one that
  parses but lies; if the 3D context is lost the same game continues in
  2D on the spot, and *try 3D again* builds a fresh renderer on a fresh
  canvas rather than asking the dead one; repeated errors offer a
  recovery card instead of a white screen, which says what happened and
  offers, in order, to try again, to try again in 2D, to clear only the
  game in progress (keeping your record), and — after naming everything
  it would take — to erase everything the room ever wrote.

the machine; without them they say so and skip what needs one, rather
than fail. `calibrate.js` is not on any list — it is a measurement, not a

strength changes.
